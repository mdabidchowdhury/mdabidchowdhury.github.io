OVERVIEW

The ±55° rule doesn’t survive contact with steel.

Netting analysis and Classical Lamination Theory point composite pressure vessels toward a winding angle near ±54.7°, the angle that balances the 2:1 hoop-to-axial stress ratio of a closed-ended cylinder. A composite repair is not a free-standing vessel, though. It is bonded over a steel pipe that carries most of the load and fails by yielding.

This study holds the inner ply at 0°, sweeps only the outer ply, and defines burst by the steel reaching its ultimate strength rather than by composite failure. Under that steel-driven criterion, unidirectional tapes peak at a low 25° helix, woven carbon peaks at a pure 90° hoop, and nothing peaks at 55°.

Abstract — Verbatim, CC BY 4.0

This study establishes a validated finite element framework for optimizing the burst capacity of petroleum-grade steel pipelines reinforced with composite overwraps. Addressing the limitations of classical netting analysis, the research develops a coupled ANSYS Mechanical and ACP workflow to systematically evaluate the influence of fiber orientation on structural integrity. The methodology first validates the static structural approach against Barlow’s equation using a thin-walled baseline (100 mm diameter, 3 mm wall), yielding a numerical failure pressure of 15.75 MPa against an analytical prediction of 13.8 MPa. Upon validation, the framework is scaled to a production-grade geometry (168.3 mm diameter, 7.1 mm wall) to execute a parametric sweep of winding angles (25 to 90 degrees) across four material systems: Epoxy E-Glass Wet, S-Glass UD, Carbon UD (230 GPa), and Woven Carbon. The results reveal a critical divergence in failure mechanics governed by fiber topology. Unidirectional systems (Carbon, S-Glass, E-Glass) consistently exhibit a “helical advantage,” achieving maximum burst pressure at 25 degrees—with Carbon UD peaking at 51.24 MPa—due to superior shear stiffening and axial confinement. Conversely, Woven Carbon displays a unique recovery trend, maximizing performance at 90 degrees (49.15 MPa) where its bidirectional architecture effectively balances hoop stresses. These findings provide a reproducible “angle–performance map” for field engineers, demonstrating that while low-angle helical wraps are optimal for high-modulus tapes, hoop-dominant configurations are required for woven fabrics, thereby refining design guidelines for in-situ pipeline rehabilitation.

An isotropic elastic–plastic steel core (von Mises yielding) is overwrapped by a thin, perfectly bonded orthotropic composite shell along the full length of a production-grade line pipe. Mesh, layup & boundary conditions

The steel uses 3D quadratic solids (SOLID186-type, ≥2 elements through the wall); the overwrap is a layered region imported from ACP. A global edge length of ~0.01 m is kept identical for every material–angle case so that comparisons stay fair.

The laminate is deliberately minimal: Ply 1 fixed at 0° (axial) and Ply 2 swept over θ ∈ {25°, 35°, 45°, 55°, 65°, 75°, 90°}, each 1 mm thick, oriented by a cylindrical rosette (0° = pipe axis, 90° = hoop). Holding everything else fixed lets the outer-ply angle be studied in isolation.

  • EndsFixed supports (axially restrained segment)
  • LoadUniform internal pressure, ramped to burst
  • InterfacePerfectly bonded — upper-bound repair
  • Ply thickness1 mm nominal × 2 plies

A steel-driven burst criterion

The fixed ends create artificial stress peaks, so the model reads stress from a central gauge region, the middle 50% of the pipe length. At each pressure step the volume-weighted average von Mises stress in the steel elements there is computed, and burst is the first pressure at which that average reaches σu:

pburst = min { p : σ̄VM(p) ≥ σu },   σu = 460 MPaSec. 3.6. This is a global yielding measure rather than a local, end-induced peak.

Composite-side failure modes (matrix cracking, fiber rupture, delamination) are intentionally not modelled. The study compares relative angle trends under one consistent steel criterion and does not claim absolute laminate strength.

Before any composite was added, the same mesh, BCs and burst criterion were run on a bare thin-walled pipe (t/D = 0.03 < 0.05) and checked against the classical closed form.

WHY IT HAPPENS

Topology sets the optimum

The same steel, geometry and criterion give opposite optimal angles depending on whether the reinforcement acts as a one-way strap or a two-way mesh.

Figure 12 — Topology-dependent optimization map

Left: the “helical advantage” of unidirectional tapes (optimum 25°). Right: the “hoop exception” of woven carbon (optimum 90°).

The helical advantage (UD, 25°)

With the inner ply locked at 0°, a 25° outer ply works as a shear-stiffening truss. It confines the pipe circumferentially while also sharing axial load, which lowers the average von Mises stress in the steel and delays yielding. A 90° UD wrap lines up with hoop stress but gives no longitudinal support, so the steel is left to take axial deformation alone and stress concentrates sooner.

The hoop exception (woven, 90°)

At 90° a woven fabric’s warp runs in the hoop direction and its weft runs along the axis, so a single ply behaves like a pre-assembled 0°/90° laminate. It gets full hoop confinement without giving up axial stiffness, which a UD tape cannot do at the same angle. That is why woven carbon recovers toward 90° while every UD system declines.

Stiffness still rules
Raw modulus sets the ceiling. Carbon UD (Ex = 121 GPa) beat both glasses at every angle, because higher stiffness lets the fibres restrain steel expansion and keep strain low. Angle tunes performance within the limit that the material allows.
Small % ≠ small deal
A 1–4% change in burst pressure translates directly into safety margin, allowable MAOP and remaining service life in pipeline rehabilitation.

LIMITATIONS & NEXT

Current limitations

  • LoadingStatic internal pressure only, with no cyclic, fatigue or thermal loads
  • InterfacePerfect bond, so results are an upper bound
  • FailureSteel yielding only; no composite damage modes
  • LaminateTwo plies (0° / θ), single thickness
  • PipePristine, with no corrosion pits or wall thinning
  • EvidenceNumerical only, with no new burst tests

Future directions

  • LoadingCyclic pressure, thermal mismatch, environmental ageing
  • InterfaceCohesive-zone models, imperfect bonding
  • FailureHashin / Tsai–Wu progressive damage
  • ArchitectureMultilayer & hybrid laminates, defected pipes
  • ValidationExperimental burst testing
  • SearchAutomated / ML-assisted material–angle selection
